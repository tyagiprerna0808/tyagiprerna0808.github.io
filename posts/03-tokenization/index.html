[Part 3 of 9] Building Blocks of Modern AI

This is Part 3 of Building Blocks of Modern AI. Before attention, before embeddings, before any gradient is computed, there is tokenization. It’s the first operation on your input and the last one on your output, and it still gets skipped in most write-ups. That’s a mistake.

The Discrete-Continuous Gap

Neural networks are continuous machines, processing floating-point numbers in high-dimensional spaces. Human language, however, is discrete. Tokenization is the bridge: the lossy, biased, and often brittle process of mapping raw strings into the integers (tokens) that a model can actually “see.”

If the tokenizer is inefficient, the model is blind to patterns, sequences become unnecessarily long, and inference costs skyrocket. And the choices are stranger than they look: by the end of this post you’ll see why the same sentence can cost a model 4–6× more tokens in Hindi than in English, a tax on meaning that nobody designed on purpose. It just fell out of how the vocabulary was built.

The evolution of the unit

The Naive Approach: Word-Level

Split on whitespace: "I love ML" \(\rightarrow\) ["I", "love", "ML"]. - The OOV Problem: What happens to "transformerification"? If it’s not in your 50,000-word vocabulary, it becomes [UNK]. You’ve lost all semantic information. - Vocabulary Explosion: To cover every morphological variation (run, running, ran), you’d need millions of embedding vectors.

The Other Extreme: Character-Level

Split every character: "hello" \(\rightarrow\) ["h", "e", "l", "l", "o"]. - Sequence Bloat: A 500-word paragraph becomes 3,000+ tokens. Given the \(O(L^2)\) cost of attention, this is computationally prohibitive. - The Burden of Spelling: The model must use its capacity to learn that h-e-l-l-o means “hello” before it can learn what “hello” means in context.

The Goldilocks Zone: Subword Tokenization

The industry standard. Common words stay whole ("the", "is"), while rare words decompose into meaningful units ("unhappiness" \(\rightarrow\) ["un", "happi", "ness"]). This ensures a fixed vocabulary size (32K–100K) with zero OOV issues.

Implementing byte-level BPE

Modern LLMs (GPT-4, Llama 3) use Byte-Level Byte-Pair Encoding.

The UTF-8 Detour

Characters in Unicode can be anything from emoji to ancient script. Starting with a vocabulary of every possible character is impossible. Instead, BBPE starts with Bytes (0-255). Every piece of text in every language is just a sequence of bytes in UTF-8.

Character Language Bytes
h English [104]
猫 Chinese [231, 140, 171]
🤖 Emoji [240, 159, 164, 150]

The Algorithm from Scratch

  1. Initialize: Convert the training corpus into its raw UTF-8 byte sequence.
  2. Count: Find the most frequent adjacent pair of tokens (initially bytes).
  3. Merge: Create a new token for that pair and replace all occurrences.
  4. Iterate: Repeat until your vocabulary size (e.g., 50,257 for GPT-2) is reached.
def get_stats(ids):
    counts = {}
    for pair in zip(ids, ids[1:]):
        counts[pair] = counts.get(pair, 0) + 1
    return counts

def merge(ids, pair, idx):
    new_ids = []
    i = 0
    while i < len(ids):
        if i < len(ids) - 1 and ids[i] == pair[0] and ids[i+1] == pair[1]:
            new_ids.append(idx)
            i += 2
        else:
            new_ids.append(ids[i])
            i += 1
    return new_ids

WordPiece: the statistical competitor

Used in BERT and Gemma, WordPiece differs from BPE in its merge criteria. BPE is greedy about frequency. WordPiece is greedy about Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI).

Instead of merging the most frequent pair, it merges the pair \((a, b)\) that maximizes: \[\text{Score}(a, b) = \frac{P(ab)}{P(a) \cdot P(b)}\]

  • Intuition: It asks, “Does this pair appear together more often than we would expect by random chance?”
  • Example: If "t" and "h" are both very common but don’t always appear as "th", their score might be lower than a rarer but highly correlated pair like "q" and "u".

Unigram: the top-down approach

Unlike the bottom-up merges of BPE/WordPiece, Unigram (used in T5 and SentencePiece) starts with a massive vocabulary and iteratively removes tokens.

At each step, it calculates how much the “loss” (likelihood of the corpus) would increase if a token were removed. It keeps the tokens that are most critical to compressing the training data. This allows for probabilistic subword segmentation: the same word can be split in multiple ways during training, which acts as a form of regularization.

The silent inequity: multilingual fragmentation

Tokenization is the root of multilingual performance gaps. Because most tokenizers are trained on English-heavy data:

  • English: "hello" \(\rightarrow\) 1 token.
  • Hindi: "नमस्ते" \(\rightarrow\) 18 bytes \(\rightarrow\) ~6 tokens.

The same meaning takes 6x more tokens in Hindi. This means the model has a shorter “effective context window” for Hindi, and Hindi users pay 6x more for the same API call. This is not a model flaw; it’s a tokenizer flaw.

Real-world tooling

In production, you don’t write your own BPE. You use: - tiktoken: OpenAI’s fast Rust-based BPE. - SentencePiece: Google’s implementation that treats whitespace as a character (escaping the need for pre-tokenization). - HuggingFace Tokenizers: The standard for open-source research.
