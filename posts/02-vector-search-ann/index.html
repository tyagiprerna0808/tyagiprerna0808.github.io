A bargain that sounds like cheating

Post 01 left us staring at a wall. Brute-force search is perfectly accurate and completely doomed: it runs out of our 10 ms budget at around 80,000 documents, and every doubling after that doubles the wait. Wikipedia has six million articles. Real corpora have billions.

So how does anyone search millions of vectors in single-digit milliseconds?

The answer, the first time you hear it, sounds like cheating: stop looking at most of the corpus.

Give up on always finding the exact nearest neighbour. Settle for almost always finding it and, in exchange, skip 99% of the comparisons. That trade has a name, Approximate Nearest Neighbour (ANN) search, and it comes down to a single dial: how much accuracy (recall) will you trade for how much speed (latency)?

The whole craft is giving up as little recall as possible. Three ideas do most of the heavy lifting, and the satisfying part is that they attack from three different angles: where to look, how cheaply to compare, and how to navigate. We’ll build all three.

Idea 1: Don’t search everywhere (IVF)

Start with the most intuitive move. Hunting for a coffee shop, you don’t scan the planet, you scan your neighbourhood. IVF (inverted file index) does exactly that to the vector space.

Before any query arrives, we carve the space into regions. Run k-means over the corpus to find \(k\) representative centres (centroids); file every document under its nearest centre. Geometrically, this tiles the space into \(k\) patches, a Voronoi partition.

Now a query comes in. Instead of scanning all \(N\) documents, we find the handful of centroids nearest the query and only search inside those tiles.

The single knob is nprobe, the number of tiles you open.

With \(k = 1{,}000\) and nprobe = 1, you look at roughly 0.1% of the corpus. Blistering, but risky: if the query lands near a tile’s edge, its true nearest neighbour might be sitting just across the border, and you’ll never see it. Turn nprobe up and you open more neighbouring tiles; latency climbs roughly linearly, recall climbs fast. That tension is the tradeoff, exposed as a number you can tune.

Idea 2: Don’t store everything (PQ)

IVF prunes where you search. But a second problem hides underneath: the vectors themselves are fat. Millions of 1,536-dimensional float vectors won’t fit in RAM, and RAM is where speed lives.

Product Quantization is a gorgeous bit of compression. The trick: stop storing vectors, start storing recipes.

Chop each vector into \(m\) short sub-vectors. (A 128-dim vector with \(m = 8\) becomes eight 16-dim chunks.) Now run k-means within each chunk’s slot across the whole corpus, and keep just 256 representative chunks per slot, a little codebook. Every sub-vector is then replaced by the single byte that names its closest codebook entry.

Do the arithmetic and it’s almost rude: a 128-float vector (512 bytes) collapses to an 8-byte code. 64× smaller.

The clever part is search: you never decompress. When a query arrives you precompute its distance to all 256 entries in each slot’s codebook, drop them in a lookup table, and then every document’s distance is just a few table lookups added together. Addition, the thing CPUs do faster than anything else.

Idea 3: Navigate, don’t scan (HNSW)

IVF and PQ are about pruning and shrinking. The last idea is about movement, and it’s the one most production vector databases actually reach for.

HNSW (hierarchical navigable small worlds) treats the corpus as a graph and turns search into a walk.

The intuition is “six degrees of separation.” In a small-world graph, hardly anyone is your direct neighbour, yet anyone is reachable in a few hops. HNSW stacks that idea into layers, like a skip-list:

  • Top layers: sparse, with long-range edges, for crossing the whole space in a few big jumps.
  • Bottom layers: dense, with short edges, for pinning down the exact neighbourhood.

Search drops in at the top, greedily hops toward the query along the long edges, then “falls” a layer and repeats with shorter ones, coarse to fine, until it’s doing a careful local search at the very bottom. You navigate to the answer instead of scanning for it.

The race

So which one wins? On the same 5,000-passage corpus from Post 01:

Algorithm Recall@10 Latency Speed-up
Brute force 1.00 0.605 ms 1×
IVF (k=100, nprobe=5) 0.94 0.082 ms 7.3×
HNSW (M=16) 0.98 0.045 ms 13.4×

Read that bottom row slowly. HNSW returned the right neighbours 98% of the time while running 13× faster than exact search, and the gap only widens as the corpus grows, which is the entire point. That’s why Pinecone, Qdrant, and most vector databases you’ve heard of default to HNSW. The catch, as always, gets paid in memory: all those graph edges have to live somewhere.

We began Post 01 unable to push past 80,000 documents. We can now, in principle, reach for the millions, having given up almost no accuracy at all.
